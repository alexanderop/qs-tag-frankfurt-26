A tech founder I know told me a story about how AI can unlock employees. His startup makes complicated software for industrial production systems. The buyers are experts, sales involve multiple stakeholders, and the product is too specialized for AI to disrupt anytime soon. Yet AI is disrupting the company's org chart.

## Trading Places: The AI-Driven Role Shift

At the founder's company, Steve led go-to-market. He knew the product well, but the usual B2B playbook was not getting enough customers in the door. What was working were the product insights Steve gathered from prospects and industry experts. He understood users so clearly that he vibe-coded several features. Some became popular product features.

Meanwhile, Edward, the product manager, struggled to articulate a clear product vision. Engineering work began to feel scattered. Edward had succeeded as a product manager elsewhere, just not in this role. But Edward was doing remarkable go-to-market work. His social posts started conversations among engineers. He built interactive demos that helped buyers see how the product solved common industrial problems differently. It was classic showing rather than telling. His superpower appeared to be communicating product value to other engineers.

The founder did something that seemed reckless three years ago. He switched their roles. Steve became product manager and started leading engineering. Edward moved into marketing, positioning, and customer development. Both felt unlocked immediately. Steve created the missing product vision, and engineers rallied around it. AI helped him prototype ideas, communicate requirements, test assumptions, and ship faster. Edward found inventive ways to reach the market because he understood the technology and its buyers.

## Beyond T-Shaped: The O-Shaped Paradigm

We hear a lot about T-shaped people, who have broad knowledge and deep expertise in one area. AI is making another shape possible. I call them O-shaped people: well-rounded, flexible people whose strongest contribution may change depending on the problem, the tools available, and the organization around them. People changing careers is nothing new. A journalist becomes a marketer. An engineer becomes a manager. A lawyer starts a company. Those moves are well documented, but they usually require leaving one box and entering another. The person retrains, earns credentials, takes a junior role, or spends years proving that experience from the old career transfers to the new one.

AI changes the mechanism by making the boundaries between professions and specialities more permeable while people are still doing the work. Someone can cross into another discipline for one task, then cross back. A marketer can prototype a feature without becoming a full-time software engineer. An engineer can build a campaign without becoming a career marketer. The switch can happen in days or hours, not after a formal reinvention.

Thus, AI accelerates "roundness" by supplying enough technical scaffolding for people to act on judgment they already possess. It translates intent into code, turns rough ideas into visual materials, explains unfamiliar concepts, and offers immediate feedback. This does not magically create taste, curiosity, or domain knowledge. But it does allow those qualities to work across siloes more easily. A person's latent ability is no longer trapped behind every skill they have not yet mastered.

The old handoff also becomes less necessary. Instead of describing an idea, submitting a request, waiting for another department, and correcting what comes back, people can make a working first version themselves. That dramatically increases the number of experiments an organization can run. It also reveals talents that conventional workflows conceal.

## Blurring Organizational Boundaries

Most companies are still organized into marketing, engineering, legal, product, and other departments. Some separation is necessary. Expertise, accountability, and safety still matter. A marketing team should not independently ship vibe-coded software that controls industrial machinery. AI does not erase the need for review or professional depth. But it does change who can initiate work and how far they can carry it. Marketers can submit product pull requests. Engineers can create interactive demos. Lawyers can prototype internal tools. Product managers can analyze data without waiting in an analytics queue. Specialists remain essential, but they increasingly guide, review, and strengthen work that begins outside their departments.

The organization's job is to create a safe canvas for this movement. Titles should describe primary responsibilities, not fence off human potential. Companies need clear review points and permissions, but also room for employees to follow insight wherever it leads. These blurred boundaries are a good thing. Most people are more capable and curious than their job descriptions allow. AI gives them a practical way to test those abilities and discover what else they can do. The companies that embrace O-shaped people will move faster and get more from the talent they already have. Employees get something equally important: the freedom to become larger than the roles they were hired to fill.
